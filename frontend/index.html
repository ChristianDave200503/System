Understand HIV

Knowledge makes room for better choices.

HIV is a virus that affects the immune system. Accurate information about transmission, testing, and treatment helps replace fear with practical support.

01 / The basics

HIV and AIDS are not the same.

HIV is the virus. AIDS is the most advanced stage of HIV infection, when the immune system has been seriously weakened. Treatment can prevent HIV from progressing to AIDS.

WHO: HIV and AIDS ↗
02 / How it spreads

Transmission happens through specific fluids.

HIV can be transmitted through blood, semen, vaginal fluids, and breast milk when the virus enters the body. Examples include condomless sex, shared injecting equipment, and transmission during pregnancy, birth, or breastfeeding without effective prevention and care.

WHO: transmission facts ↗
03 / What does not spread it

Everyday contact is safe.

HIV is not transmitted by hugging, handshakes, sharing meals or toilets, or mosquitoes. People living with HIV deserve the same welcome, friendship, and respect as anyone else.

WHO: common myths ↗
Symptoms cannot tell you your HIV status.

Some people have flu-like symptoms soon after acquiring HIV; others have no noticeable symptoms. Many other conditions can cause similar symptoms. Testing—not guessing—is how to know. If you have health concerns, contact a qualified healthcare professional.

Prevention that respects your choices

There is more than one way to prevent HIV.

People have different relationships, bodies, and circumstances. A healthcare professional can help you choose an approach that fits your needs; combining methods can provide additional protection.

01 / Barrier methods

Condoms and lubricant

Correct, consistent use of condoms helps prevent HIV and many other sexually transmitted infections. Water- or silicone-based lubricant can reduce friction and the chance that a condom breaks. Condoms also help prevent pregnancy, though they do not address every sexual-health need.

WHO: HIV prevention ↗
02 / Before exposure

PrEP is a prevention medicine.

Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is medicine for people who do not have HIV and may be exposed to it. It is highly effective when taken as prescribed. A clinician will arrange an HIV test and follow-up checks; PrEP does not prevent other STIs or pregnancy.

CDC: PrEP overview ↗
03 / After a possible exposure

PEP is time-sensitive.

Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) is emergency medicine after a possible HIV exposure. It must be started as soon as possible and within 72 hours. A healthcare professional can assess the exposure and provide next steps. PEP is usually taken for 28 days when prescribed.

CDC: PEP overview ↗
04 / Safer health services

Use sterile equipment.

Never share needles, syringes, or other injecting equipment. Use sterile equipment and seek health services that follow infection-prevention standards. Health workers should follow standard precautions with every patient—HIV status should never determine whether someone receives safe care.

WHO: prevention and transmission ↗
!

Think you may have been exposed in the last 72 hours?

Seek urgent medical care now and ask about PEP. Do not wait for symptoms or for an online answer—the sooner PEP starts, the better. This page cannot assess an individual exposure.

Testing, treatment & living well

Knowing your status connects you to choices.

HIV testing is voluntary and should be confidential. Testing services can help people access prevention or, if a test confirms HIV, treatment and ongoing support.

01

Choose a trusted testing service.

Ask a qualified health facility, clinic, or community-based service about confidential testing. WHO also supports HIV self-testing as an additional option; a reactive self-test is a screening result, not a confirmed diagnosis.

02

Follow the testing process.

Testing may involve a rapid or laboratory test. A trained provider follows the nationally approved testing algorithm and arranges confirmatory testing if needed. The right time to test after an exposure depends on the test and situation—ask the provider whether repeat testing is recommended.

03

If HIV is confirmed, treatment works.

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) controls HIV by reducing the amount of virus in the body. With continued treatment and care, people living with HIV can live long, healthy lives. Starting and staying on treatment is a personal healthcare journey, supported by a qualified provider.

04

Undetectable means no sexual transmission.

When a person takes HIV treatment and maintains an undetectable viral load, they do not transmit HIV through sex (U=U). A healthcare provider can explain viral-load monitoring and what the result means for each person's care.

Looking for testing or treatment in the Philippines?

Contact a licensed healthcare facility or your local health office and ask about confidential HIV testing, accredited treatment hubs, and available prevention services. Confirm service availability and requirements directly with the provider.

Gender, power & access

Equality changes the HIV response.

Gender is not a risk category. Inequity is a barrier—and communities can remove it together. UNAIDS reports that women and girls accounted for 44% of new HIV acquisitions in 2025; treatment access also differed between adult women (84%) and men (74%). Gender-based violence, stigma, and unequal decision-making can also make prevention and care harder to reach. See the 2025 data ↗ · Gender and HIV ↗

01 / Agency

Everyone deserves choices.

Unequal power in relationships can make it harder to negotiate safer sex or access contraception and testing. Consent, education, and services that respect autonomy help put people in control.

02 / Safety

Safety is part of health.

Gender-based violence and fear of discrimination can keep people from seeking care. Confidential, affirming services—and communities that believe and support survivors—make care more reachable.

03 / Inclusion

Care must fit real lives.

Women and girls, men and boys, and transgender and gender-diverse people are not one-size-fits-all groups. Respectful care listens to each person and protects their privacy and dignity.

04 / Solidarity

Stigma hurts the whole response.

Blame and stereotypes discourage honest conversations and timely testing. Use person-first language, challenge misinformation, and make space for people living with HIV to lead.

Rights make care possible

Privacy, respect, and care are not privileges.

In the Philippines, the HIV and AIDS Policy Act sets protections and responsibilities for a rights-based national response. Laws work best when people know them and institutions uphold them.

  • Protect the confidentiality of HIV-related information.
  • Prohibit discrimination based on actual, perceived, or suspected HIV status.
  • Support access to HIV education, prevention, testing, and treatment services.

This is a brief, non-legal summary—not legal advice. Read the full law and consult a qualified professional about a specific situation.

Philippines · Republic Act No. 11166

Philippine HIV and AIDS Policy Act

Enacted in 2018, RA 11166 provides the framework for HIV education, prevention, treatment, care, support, and protection from HIV-related discrimination, while safeguarding confidentiality.
